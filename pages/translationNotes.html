"Primi Dies Terrae"

For the most part Primi Dies Terrae is the most
historically accurate book, since the first,
second, and third generations of Antioch all
kept entire journals for each member,
allowing easy translation and third person
narratives to be constructed through
the eyewitness accounts.

For example, from Lauren’s journal comes
the best account of what happened to
Tyler and Sebastian:
“Hodie Tyler et Sebastianus acerrime inter
se pugnaverunt, nescio qua de causa.
Nemo scit cur id fecerint,
sed pugna fuit vehementissima et letalis!
Tandem Tyler Sebastianum interfecit;
quo interfecto, ipse quoque modo arcano
mortuus est—sed qua de causa,
adhuc ignoramus.”

The literal translation reads as:
“Today Tyler and Sebastian fought each
other fiercely, for some unknown reason.
No one knows why they did it,
but the fight was extremely violent
and deadly! Finally, Tyler killed
Sebastian; once Sebastian was killed,
Tyler himself also died in a mysterious
way—but the cause remains unknown.”

The context of what happened to Tyler
and his mysterious death were added
from a fifth generation church scroll
describing the encounter’s aftermath
in detail: “Dominus Cedar raro rebus
humanis se immiscere volebat,
sed tandem statuit satis damni iam
illatum esse. Nam Tyler mortuus erat
atque in agris iuxta Sebastianum
iacebat; quem Dominus Cedar sustulit
et in tempus futurum transtulit.
Dixitque Dominus Cedar: "Facinus
nefarium admisisti quod non praevideram.
Scelus tuum crudeliter punietur,
sicut facta tua crudelia fuerunt."
Tum Dominus Cedar poenam aeternam constituit,
quam omnes malefici post mortem subirent.
Itaque Tyler reliquum aeternitatis intra
arborem degit, ibi inclusus atque intolerabili
pollinis irritatione vexatus.”

Directly translated, this becomes:
“Lord Cedar rarely wished to involve himself
in human affairs, but at last he decided
that enough harm had been done.
For Tyler was dead and lay in the fields
beside Sebastian; Lord Cedar lifted him up
and transported him into the future.
And Lord Cedar said: "You have committed a heinous
deed that I did not foresee.
Your crime shall be punished cruelly,
just as your deeds were cruel."
Then Lord Cedar decreed an eternal punishment
that all evildoers would undergo after death.
And so Tyler spent the rest of eternity inside a tree,
confined there and tormented by the unbearable
irritation of pollen.”

This is a near exact description and translation
of the more direct and easier to read version
presented in chapter 3 of Primi Dies Terrae.
Only a few words such as moving Tyler
into the Future make little sense,
and were rectified based upon sense
and other sources.

Note:
Throughout the texts,
Cedrus uses He/it pronouns,
but literal translations often move towards
He/Him or It/Its. The combination of sources
leads to the combination of different
references towards Cedrus’s identity and its pronouns.

"Divinae Leges"

The lorekeeper wrote exactly in the original
language, “Prophetae verbum divinum acceperunt
atque nobis protulerunt, verbum Domini Cedri
perfecte in chartam quam habeo atque in mentes
nostras transferentes — carmen scilicet
magistrale Domini nostri divini.”

Or roughly translated to english: “The prophets
received the divine word from Cedrus and
proclaimed it to us, perfectly transcribing
the word of The Lord Cedar onto the paper
I hold and write on currently and into our
minds—the master-poetry, indeed, of our
divine Lord.”

The original scrolls were mostly worship
procedures and logs of the church meetings,
but they contained the direct word, so it
was put onto the paper and sufficiently
translated into a teachable and wonderful
guide such as the one in your palm.

For example, the original writing of
Divinae Leges Chapter 2 Verse Six is this:
“Et propheta dixit: ‘Numquam arbori, frutici
vel virgulto damnum inferetis sub poena
mortis; hoc ad omnes plantas pertinet,
nam eas potius colere quam laedere debetis.’”

Literally translated it equates to:
“And the prophet said: ‘You shall never
inflict harm upon a tree, a shrub, or a
sapling, under penalty of death; this applies
to all plants, for you ought to cultivate
them rather than harm them.’"

This can be translated into Divinae Leges 2:6
“You shall not ever harm a tree, bush, or
shrub under penalty of death, this applies
for all plants, for you shall worship them
rather than injure them.”

The concept of worship of the plants comes
from other church documents direct translations,
such as “Plantas cole; ita Cedrus iubet.”,
meaning “Worship plants; so the Cedar commands.”
Clearly “the Cedar” is Cedrus, The Lord Cedar,
in the context, but literal translations often
do not carry the exact perfect details of the
church meetings and documents.

"Malum Surgit"

The Lorekeeper wrote down the first encounter
with Mariana in detail, as shown here:

“Subito terra dehiscit, luce rubra inde effusa.
Clamores ex hiatu exaudire poteram atque
strepitum tumultuosum ac confusum. Tum subito
mulier procerissima, veste longa sericaque
rubra induta, cuius caput corona cornuum ornata
erat, ex illo terrae hiatu coram nobis apparuit.”

Directly translated the text becomes:

“Suddenly the earth split open, and a red light
poured forth from the opening. I could hear
shouts coming from the chasm, along with a
tumultuous and confused din. Then, all at once,
a very tall woman—clad in a long, red silk robe
and wearing a crown of horns—appeared before us
from that opening in the ground.”

This becomes the version seen in Malum Surgit.

Further changes and additions come in the case
of the finding of the tomb, which Soren describes
in the original latin like this:

“Tandem ruinas antiquas invenimus atque ad locum
medium, quem Orion indicaverat, accessimus.
Cedrum marcidam conspexi; itaque genibus nixus
Domino Cedro pro salute eius supplicavi. Deinde
circum Cedrum et subter eam terram fodimus;
atque, ut Orion praedixerat, decem pedes infra
saxum exstabat. Circumfodientes aditum tandem
repperimus et in eum desiluimus. Sepulcrum forma
hexagonali erat, ossibus pulvereque ubique
sparsis; quattuorque ibi sarcophagi stabant,
in quorum corporibus conservatis vulnera,
ictibus telorum illata videntia, apparebant.”

Directly translated this turns into:

“At last, we discovered the ancient ruins and
approached the central spot that Orion had
pointed out. I caught sight of a withered cedar;
kneeling down, I prayed to the Lord Cedar for
its well-being. Then we dug up the earth around
and beneath the tree; and, just as Orion had
predicted, a stone structure lay exposed ten
feet down. After digging around it, we finally
found an entrance and jumped inside. It was a
hexagonal tomb, with bones and dust scattered
everywhere; four sarcophagi stood there, and on
the preserved bodies, wounds inflicted by weapon
blows were clearly visible.”

The text written on the wall of the tomb is also
slightly different in original form and direct
translation:

“Noctu impetum fecit, cives nostros temere
trucidans. Cultro longo armata quattuor homines
dormientes confodit, de lectis in humum frigidam
calcibus deiecit, atque eos prae metu clamantes
dum moriebantur spectavit. Clamores audientes
omnes irrupimus et rem atrocem conspeximus; tum
ei, Marianae, diximus: "Cur hoc facinus tam
nefandum perpetrasti?

Illa respondit: "Malum me devoravit; nam sonum
quendam audivi. Pulsatio erat quae paulatim in
verba mutata est; verba autem iubebant ut eos
occiderem, quo facto pulsatio cessaret—et nunc
cessavit! Pulsatio cessavit! Nonne audivistis
illam pulsationem terribilem, continuam, foedam,
strepitosam, vehementem?"

Tunc intelleximus eam viribus malis succubuisse;
nam plantam ut scelesta laesisse debuit. Haec
opinio confirmata est cum postridie prima luce
arborem succisam extra vicum nostrum invenimus,
securi eius iuxta iacente.

Sciebamus eam periculosam esse, nam in nos quoque
impetum fecit vitamque nostram petivit; nos autem
eam in templum vetus sub cedro coniecimus atque
ibi, alte sub terra, inclusimus.

Tamen effugit; nam postridie eam in cedro
sedentem, securi in manu, invenimus. Tum cedrum
securi caedere coepit, facinus atrox perpetrans.
Sciebamus eam perfidam esse, itaque fugere
constituimus. Pars nostra—ego quoque—cum cibariis
in templum confugit atque nos in sepulcro inter
corpora mortuorum inclusimus. Corpora in loculos
sollemnes posuimus, sperantes ea aliquando
inventum iri et rite sepultum iri. Ceteri autem
tribulis versus meridiem cum sarcinis ciborum
potuumque fugerunt. Opto modo ut locum idoneum
ad habitandum inveniant atque a metu Marianae
tuti sint. Si di volent, vivi ex hoc sepulcro
exire poterimus.”

Translated directly the text becomes:

“She launched an attack by night, recklessly
slaughtering our fellow citizens. Armed with a
long knife, she stabbed four sleeping people,
kicked them from their beds onto the cold ground,
and watched as they screamed in terror while
dying. Hearing the cries, we all burst in and
witnessed the atrocity; then we asked her,
Mariana: "Why did you commit such a heinous crime?

She replied: "An evil force consumed me; for I
heard a certain sound. It was a pounding that
gradually turned into words; and the words
commanded me to kill them so that the pounding
would cease—and now it has ceased! The pounding
has stopped! Did you not hear that terrible,
ceaseless, foul, clamorous, and violent pounding?

Then we understood that she had succumbed to
evil forces; for she must have harmed the plant,
an act of wickedness. This belief was confirmed
when, at first light the next day, we found the
tree cut down outside our village, with her axe
lying beside it.

We knew she was dangerous, for she had attacked
us too and sought our lives; so we cast her into
the old temple beneath the cedar tree and
imprisoned her deep underground.

Yet she escaped; for the next day we found her
sitting in the cedar tree, axe in hand. Then she
began to hack at the cedar with the axe,
committing a monstrous deed. We knew she was
treacherous, so we decided to flee. Some of
us—myself included—took provisions and fled into
the temple, shutting ourselves inside the tomb
amidst the bodies of the dead. We placed the
bodies in ceremonial coffins, hoping they would
one day be found and given a proper burial. The
rest of the tribesmen, however, fled toward the
south with their packs of food and drink. I only
hope they find a suitable place to live and
remain safe from the threat of Mariana. If the
gods will it, we shall be able to emerge alive
from this tomb.”

The text was slightly adjusted for clarity,
grammar, and readability, but this text stays
mostly the same.

The description of First Church of Holy Cedar
in the original language is:

“Ecclesia formam hexagoni prae se ferebat, ad
illuminationem eorum significandam qui in
hexagono exteriore sedentes Cedrum in hexagono
interiore despiciebant — arborem quam Prophetae
et Episcopi, gradibus insistentes, circumibant
ut ad sectatores loquerentur.”

Directly translated this becomes:

“The church took the form of a hexagon,
symbolizing the enlightenment of those who,
seated in the outer hexagon, looked down upon
The Cedar in the inner hexagon—a tree around
which Prophets and Bishops, standing upon raised
steps, would gather to address their followers.”
